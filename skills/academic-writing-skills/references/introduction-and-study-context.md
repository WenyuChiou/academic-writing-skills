## Scope and Development Order

Use this guide for the reader functions of an Introduction or study-context section, even when the manuscript uses another heading. Follow the author's declared development order. For an empirical article, a useful starting sequence is Introduction → study context → Methods → Results → Discussion → Conclusion → rebuilding the Abstract from the stabilized text. This is not a required structure for every genre. Revisit earlier framing when later evidence changes what the study can answer.

Before drafting, identify the research purpose, design, intended audience, available sources, and relevant study facts. Missing selection rationales, source support, or scope details are unknowns to resolve, not invitations to invent them. Apply the common paragraph and reader-guidance rules in [universal-integrity.md](universal-integrity.md) and [prose-and-citation-editing.md](prose-and-citation-editing.md).

Use the functions below to plan the requested unit. They may be combined, distributed across several paragraphs, or reordered when a different logical relation serves the reader. Do not turn the tables into four-paragraph templates, require numbered questions, or expand a local edit into a whole-paper review.

## Introduction

The Introduction establishes why the study is needed and how its purpose follows from the available knowledge.

| Reader function | Drafting decision | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Establish the problem and its importance | Begin at a level that leads directly to the actual problem; identify the understanding or decision at stake. | A broad opening whose relevance to this study appears only much later. |
| Establish what is known | Organize studies by the relationship that advances the argument; use close precedents to establish what has already been addressed. | An inventory of individual papers or omission of overlapping work to manufacture novelty. |
| Specify the gap and why it matters | Name the unresolved knowledge, comparison, population, scale, or analytical level and its justified consequence. | An unspecified “remains unclear,” an unsupported “no study,” or generic claims of importance. |
| State the study's purpose and approach | Derive objectives or questions from the gap; give the minimum high-level approach and evidence-bounded intended contribution. | Questions unrelated to the gap, implementation detail, or a contribution stronger than the design can support. |

Introduce the concepts or institutional background needed to understand the problem before using them to motivate group differences or research questions. Do not assume that readers already know why a named distinction matters. Keep detailed site description and reproducible procedures in the sections that perform those functions.

At section scope, test whether the reader can identify the problem, existing knowledge, missing piece, consequence of that gap, and the study's response. Read paragraph openings and endings together: they should advance the argument rather than repeatedly announce importance or novelty. A paragraph-level task checks its contribution to that chain and available neighboring text, not every function in the full Introduction.

## Study Area, Context, and Evidence Setting

This section establishes the setting needed to interpret the design, the reason for using it, and the actual scope of the evidence. It is not a general geographic or demographic encyclopedia. In a non-geographic study, the corresponding setting may be a population, institution, dataset, experimental environment, or case.

| Reader function | Drafting decision | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Describe relevant context | Select geographic, social, institutional, or sampling background that helps readers understand the research problem or design. | Background facts with no role in the subsequent study. |
| Explain selection | State the actual reason for using the site, population, dataset, or case, including practical data availability when relevant. | Inferring that a selected case is ideal, typical, or representative without supporting evidence. |
| Delimit the evidence | Identify the actual spatial, temporal, and population scope at the level needed for interpretation. | Equating a whole region with the analytical domain or a target population with the realized sample. |
| Supply prerequisites for Methods | Explain the background concepts or institutional distinctions needed to understand later settings. | Premature parameters or algorithms, or an implication that a real-world rule is implemented by the study merely because it is described here. |

Distinguish the broad setting, the analytical domain or sampling frame, and the units actually observed or generated. For surveys, distinguish the target population, eligible population or frame, and realized sample where relevant. For simulations, distinguish observed contextual facts from synthetic assignments. Selection rationale does not establish representativeness or broad generalizability.

Relevant institutional background may extend beyond what the study implements. Explain its role without implying that all such rules are modeled or enforced; describe the actual representation and material omissions in Methods and Limitations as needed. At this stage, background explains why a distinction matters; Methods explains how the study operationalizes it.

At section scope, test whether the reader knows the relevant setting, why it was selected, what was actually studied, and enough background to follow Methods. Keep reviewed maps, captions, tables, and descriptions consistent. Do not add a results-style “so what” or an RQ label solely to close the section.

## First-Three-Section Handoffs

Treat Introduction, study context, and Methods as a reader's chain: **why this question → which setting and evidence scope → how the study can answer it**. Test the handoffs, not compliance with three headings or a fixed paragraph order.

| Handoff | Check the relation |
|---|---|
| Introduction → study context | Does the actual case or population fit the question, and is its selection rationale stated without manufacturing representativeness? |
| Study context → Methods | Are the concepts needed for the procedure introduced, and is it clear which contextual facts are measured, synthetically assigned, or only background? |
| Methods → the stated question | Working backward from the planned outputs, can the procedure produce the kind of evidence the question requires, at the stated scope and inference level? |

Track the entity or unit, concept or outcome, spatial and temporal scope, and permitted inference through the relevant handoff. Differences can be legitimate: external survey data, for example, can inform a case model without being observations of the modeled households. State the actual role and any transfer assumption rather than forcing sources to have identical coverage or silently treating them as interchangeable.

The same concept has different jobs in these sections. Introduction explains why it matters; study context establishes its local relevance and conditions; Methods states its actual representation, settings, and material omissions. Refer back briefly rather than repeat the full explanation. For operational provenance, use [methods-and-assumptions.md](methods-and-assumptions.md).

## Hidden Mismatch Checks

Use these questions when the claim or design makes them relevant. They expose recognizable forms of hidden mismatch; they are not an exhaustive detector of unknown unknowns or reasons to rewrite otherwise functioning sections.

| Where a problem can hide | Diagnostic question and possible repair |
|---|---|
| A gap described with new labels | Does a close precedent address substantially the same task under other terminology? Compare the actual question, outcome, scale, and evidence before asserting novelty. |
| A broad purpose and a narrower measured outcome | Is the question about welfare, causal mechanisms, or population behavior while the planned evidence concerns modeled asset loss or a descriptive association? State the supported task; do not rename the output to imply the broader construct was measured. |
| Case selection and data availability | Could selection or missing coverage restrict what can be learned? Report the actual selection process and relevant boundary; do not invent a bias, declare invalidity, or claim representativeness merely from coverage. |
| A map or contextual percentage | Is an area-based quantity being used as a household- or population-based quantity? Identify any mapping or assignment assumption instead of silently equating the units. |
| Institutional background becoming a model claim | Are a rule's conditions preserved, and does the reader know whether the study represents eligibility, compliance, enforcement, or none of these? A simplified representation can be legitimate if its role and boundary are clear. |

When a mismatch appears, identify the exact missing link, the source needed to resolve it, and the smallest authorized repair. If resolution would require new data, a changed question, or a different model, flag that decision rather than quietly supplying it through prose.

## Placement and Revision Checks

Place content according to its function, not just its topic:

- background necessary to understand the research problem → Introduction;
- case-specific context and actual evidence scope → study context or the corresponding setting section;
- assignments, parameters, data transformations, sampling procedures, and algorithms → Methods or the genre's procedural section.

The same concept may appear in several sections for different purposes; do not repeat its full explanation each time. Respect a genre or venue that combines these functions under different headings.

For a new draft, plan functions and evidence before choosing paragraph boundaries. For an accepted, submitted, or advisor-edited draft, locate the missing or misplaced function and make the smallest defensible repair. Do not rebuild functioning paragraphs merely to match this guide. If content must move, name its destination and check the affected transitions and references.

Use [lifecycle-and-routing.md](lifecycle-and-routing.md) for scope and change-impact checks. A changed gap or research question can affect the evidence chain and summaries; a local syntax repair does not authorize rewriting them.
